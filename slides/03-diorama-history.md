## A short history of the diorama

- **1822**: Louis Daguerre (later of photography) opens the **Diorama** in Paris: huge paintings that change with the light
- **1890**: Carl Akeley's **muskrat group** in Milwaukee: real animals in a 3D scene that fades into a painted backdrop
- **1940s**: Frances Glessner Lee's **Nutshell Studies**: tiny crime scenes, still used to train detectives
- **1976 on**: Hiroshi Sugimoto photographs museum dioramas until they pass for the real world
- **Today**: low-poly worlds in Blender and the browser, on a plinth

???

Diorama comes from the Greek for "seeing through". Daguerre's Diorama was a theatre: the audience
sat still while lighting changed huge translucent paintings from day to night.

Akeley's "Milwaukee style" muskrat group (1890, still on show at the Milwaukee Public Museum)
set the pattern for natural history halls: real things in front, fading into a painted curve.

Glessner Lee built 20 Nutshell Studies; 18 survive and are still used in forensic training.

Why dioramas work: a bounded world, and you're a giant looking in. The cut edge and the plinth
say "this is a made thing", and that invites you to look closely. Workbook 3's walls and plinth
are exactly this.
